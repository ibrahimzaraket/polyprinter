Definitions

Plain-language explanations for the jargon used elsewhere on this site. No trading background assumed. Jump here anytime by clicking a ? next to a column header or field label.

Performance — did they actually make money?
ROI (return on investment / return on capital deployed)
How much profit a trader made, as a percentage of the money they put in. A trader who turned $1,000 into $1,220 of profit-and-capital has a 22% ROI.
Shrinkage / shrunk ROI
A correction that pulls a trader's ROI toward the average, more strongly when they've made few trades. A trader who is 300% up after only 6 trades has probably just been lucky — 6 trades isn't enough evidence to trust the number as-is. A trader who is 22% up after 400 trades has proven it over and over. Without this correction, the leaderboard would be dominated by lucky beginners instead of consistently good traders. The formula treats every trader as if they'd also played roughly 30 "average" trades in addition to their real ones, so a small sample gets pulled hard toward the middle and a large sample barely moves.
Why it matters: this is the whole reason the ranked Traders list isn't just "biggest number wins" — it's what stops a 6-trade lucky streak from outranking a proven track record.
Win rate
Of the trades that have finished (resolved), what percentage were profitable. 80% win rate means 8 out of every 10 resolved trades made money.
Average win / average loss / win-loss ratio
Average win is the typical dollar profit on a winning trade; average loss is the typical dollar loss on a losing trade. Win/loss ratio divides one by the other — a ratio of 2.0 means their typical win is twice the size of their typical loss. A trader can have a low win rate and still be profitable if their wins are much bigger than their losses (or vice versa), which is why these numbers are shown together, not alone.
Profit concentration (top-1 / top-5)
What share of a trader's total profit came from just their single best trade ("top 1"), or from their five best trades ("top 5"). If top-1 is 90%, almost all of their reported profit rides on one lucky market — the rest of their track record barely matters.
Why it matters: a trader with low concentration made their money consistently, across many trades. A trader with high concentration made it once and got carried by that one result — much riskier to copy going forward.
Copyability — can we actually follow this trader in practice?
Hold-to-resolution rate
Of the positions a trader closed, what share did they hold all the way until the market resolved (rather than selling early for a quick exit).
Why it matters: this is called the single most predictive field in the whole dossier. If a trader's edge comes from exiting at exactly the right moment, we can't copy that — by the time we see their trade and react, that moment has passed. A trader who mostly holds to resolution is one whose edge survives our lag; a trader who mostly trades out early has an edge we structurally can't copy.
Median / p90 holding period
How long a trader typically keeps a position open, in hours, before closing it. "Median" is the middle value (half their trades are shorter, half longer). "p90" is the 90th percentile — the length of their longer holds, excluding only their most extreme 10%.
Entry price distribution / deciles
Prediction-market prices range from 1¢ to 99¢ and roughly represent the probability the market assigns to an outcome. This shows the spread of prices a trader typically buys at: their p10 (a low-end price — 10% of their trades enter cheaper than this), their median (the middle), and their p90 (a high-end price).
Why it matters: a trader who mostly buys sub-10¢ longshots is chasing big payouts on unlikely outcomes. That style is effectively uncopyable at a small bankroll — the position sizes needed to make it worthwhile, and the number of losing bets you sit through along the way, don't fit a $1,000 paper account, regardless of whether the trader's overall math works out for them.
Sizing pattern (coefficient of variation)
Whether a trader bets roughly the same dollar amount every time ("flat" sizing) or varies their bet size a lot depending on how confident they are ("conviction" sizing). Measured as the coefficient of variation (CV) of their position sizes — a low number means flat, a high number means conviction-sized.
Why it matters: conviction sizing is a signal worth copying (they're telling you which trades they believe in most); flat sizing means bet size carries no extra information.
Category Copy Score
An A–D letter grade computed separately for each category a trader trades in (Sports, Crypto, Politics, and so on) — a trader can be sharp in one category and mediocre in another, and one blended dossier number hides that. Computed as a plain formula, not an LLM opinion: category win rate (shrunk toward the trader's own overall win rate so a lucky 4-trade streak in one category doesn't read as an A), sample depth (fewer than 3 resolved positions in a category isn't graded at all; fewer than 8 caps the grade at C), entry price quality (extreme prices near 0¢ or 100¢ are hard to size and fill correctly), P&L concentration (one big win carrying the whole category record scores lower than a consistent spread), and sizing "conviction" (does bet size vary meaningfully with confidence, or is every bet flat-sized). Category itself comes from Polymarket's own event tags (fetched from gamma-api, cached once per market) — not a title-keyword guess.
Why it matters: a trader worth following in NBA player props might be a coin flip in politics. Sizing and mandate decisions made off one lifetime ROI number treat every category the same; this doesn't.
Liveness — is this trader (and this system) still working?
Liveness
Whether a trader is still actively trading — based on recent trade counts and whether they currently hold any open positions. A trader can have a great historical dossier and still be inactive (stopped trading).
Proxy wallet
On Polymarket, the wallet address that shows up on the public leaderboard and in trade history isn't always a person's main crypto wallet — it's usually a "proxy" wallet the Polymarket app creates and controls on their behalf. All the trader addresses tracked in this dashboard are these proxy wallets, which is normal and expected, not a red flag.
Heartbeat / stale heartbeat
Each background service (Scout, Mirror, the dashboard itself) periodically "checks in" with a timestamp so the system can tell it's still alive. A heartbeat is "stale" if a service hasn't checked in for more than 2 minutes — that usually means the service has crashed or hung, not that there's simply nothing to do right now.
Why it matters: without this distinction, a dead service and a quiet market look identical — both show "nothing happening." A stale heartbeat is the difference between "all is well, we're just waiting" and "something broke."
System — how this dashboard itself works
Paper mode vs. live mode
Paper mode simulates trades with fake money to test the strategy without financial risk. Live mode would place real trades with real capital. The badge in the top-left of every page shows which mode the system is currently running in — this dashboard is currently in PAPER mode, meaning no real money is at risk.
Decision, TAKE vs. SKIP
Every trade a followed trader makes produces exactly one "decision" row: TAKE means the system copied it (in paper or live mode); SKIP means it deliberately chose not to, along with a specific reason (for example: the trader has no active mandate, the market's liquidity was too low, or the portfolio was already at its exposure cap). Every observed trade gets a decision — skipping is a normal, logged outcome, not an error.
Copy tax
The cost of being a follower instead of the original trader — the gap between the price they got and the price we got, because we're always reacting a little after them. Measured in cents of price (not percent), because on a prediction market the price already behaves like a probability out of $1, so a flat cents number is the honest way to measure the gap.
Why it matters: this is the core viability number for the whole project. If copy tax regularly eats more than the edge the trader actually had, copying them mechanically loses money even when they themselves are profitable.
Neg-risk market
A "negative risk" market is a Polymarket structure used for questions with several mutually exclusive outcomes (e.g. "who wins the election" with many candidates), where the underlying token mechanics are more complex than a simple two-sided Yes/No market. They're called out separately because that complexity makes trades on them easier to misread as something they're not.
